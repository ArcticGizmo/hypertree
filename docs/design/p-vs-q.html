Two ways to jump into a worktree scope

Same journey, same intent — step through it in both models and feel where they diverge. P gives the scope its own depth axis you dive into and surface out of. Q splices the scope inline into one continuous strip. Watch the crux steps (3 and 4).

Day-to-day desktop feat-123 worktree scope Focus — where you are right now

PDive / Surface

The scope lives on a second axis. Down dives in, up surfaces out. Left/right stays scoped to the level you're on.

QInline expand

The scope splices into your one horizontal strip. Only ever left/right — no modes, but the strip grows and the scope has no edges.

How you'd drive it

You keep the horizontal muscle memory you already have. Model P just adds one vertical axis for depth — the two amber keys below are the whole difference from today.

Today Flat desktops
Win+Ctrl+←/→ step along one long line of desktops

One axis. Day-to-day desktops and every worktree sit in the same flat row — the more worktrees, the longer the line you scan.

Model P Dive & surface
Win+←/→ move within the level you're on
Win+↓ dive into the scope anchored here
Win+↑ surface back out to your anchor

Same left/right as today — plus a new vertical dimension. Depth is a felt state, and surfacing always returns you to the exact desktop you dived from.

Optional fourth binding worth reserving: Win+Ctrl+↓ to hop straight from one scope into another without surfacing first — a “scope switcher” for when you're jumping between two worktrees a lot.

P What dive / surface buys you

  • A boundary you can feel — you always know if you're inside a scope or out in day-to-day.
  • Resumable: diving back lands you exactly where you left off inside the scope.
  • Anchored in space — “feat-123 lives under my Web desktop.”
  • Cost: one extra keystroke to cross the boundary, and a depth mode to hold in your head.

Q What inline expand buys you

  • One axis only — left/right forever, no modes to track.
  • Fluid: flow straight from inside the scope out into day-to-day without popping.
  • Fewer keystrokes at the edges of a scope.
  • Cost: the strip keeps growing, and nothing marks where a scope begins or ends — no scope memory.

These bindings assume Win+arrow is free so the new vertical axis reads cleanly against today's flat Win+Ctrl+←/→. In reality Win+arrow is Windows' window-snap, so a real build would land on something like Win+Ctrl+arrow or a custom hotkey layer — the shape (add up/down for depth) is what matters, not the exact keys. And the on-screen HUD chip is doing real work: native Task View can't draw a second axis, so that small “where am I” readout is what actually keeps you un-lost.
